# Sentence Generation Standards
## Cross-Linguistic Back-Translation Fidelity Analysis Project

---

## Project Context

This document defines the standards for generating domain-specific English sentences used in a cross-linguistic back-translation fidelity study. The study evaluates translation quality across 40+ languages spanning 8 language families, using three translation systems (Google Translate, MyMemory, LibreTranslate) and three evaluation metrics (Cosine Similarity, BLEU, TER).

Sentences are translated from English into a target language and then back to English. The back-translated result is compared against the original using automated metrics. The quality of the sentences directly affects the validity of the research findings, so consistency and intentionality in sentence construction is critical.

---

## Universal Standards (All Domains)

These rules apply to every sentence regardless of domain.

**Language:**
- American English only
- No British spellings (use: color, realize, analyze, center)
- Natural spoken or written register appropriate to the domain
- No named individuals, specific brands, or trademarked terms
- No proper nouns that anchor meaning to a specific place unless the cultural domain requires it

**Structure:**
- 8 to 25 words per sentence, sweet spot is 12 to 18
- One clear idea per sentence — do not combine two thoughts
- No compound sentences joined by semicolons
- Varied sentence length across the dataset — do not write all sentences at the same length
- Varied sentence structure — mix declarative, conditional, and negative constructions

**Content:**
- No duplicate or near-duplicate sentences
- No sentences that overlap heavily in meaning with another sentence in the same file
- Varied topics within each domain — do not cluster around one subject
- Read every sentence aloud before finalizing — if it sounds unnatural, rewrite it

**What to avoid:**
- Sentences that are too generic to belong to a specific domain
- Sentences so culturally niche that only a small subset of Americans would recognize them
- Overly complex vocabulary that obscures meaning rather than adding precision
- Sentences over 25 words — long sentences introduce translation artifacts unrelated to domain difficulty

## Domain Standards

---

### Conversational

**Purpose:**
Test how well translators preserve the natural, informal register of everyday spoken American English. This domain targets casual speech patterns, contractions, hedging language, and the kind of back-and-forth phrasing found in text messages and informal conversation.

**Register:** Informal, casual, spoken-first

**Must include:**
- Contractions (I'm, don't, can't, we'll, that's)
- Natural filler or hedge phrases (honestly, kind of, I guess, not gonna lie)
- A mix of positive, negative, and uncertain statements
- Questions and responses that feel like real dialogue
- First and second person constructions

**Must avoid:**
- Formal or professional language
- Technical vocabulary of any kind
- Idioms (reserved for idiomatic domain)
- Slang so niche or regional it would not be recognized nationally (e.g. heavy AAVE, deep regional dialect)
- Sentences that sound written rather than spoken

**Good examples:**
- I didn't mean to come off as rude earlier.
- Are we still on for tonight, or did plans change?
- I'm running a bit late, sorry.
- That makes sense now that you explain it like that.
- I totally forgot about that, my bad.

**Bad examples:**
- I was unable to attend the scheduled meeting due to unforeseen circumstances. (too formal)
- Yo that was wild lmaooo (too niche, won't translate meaningfully)
- I went to the store and then came home and made dinner. (run-on, two ideas)

---

### Cultural

**Purpose:**
Test how well translators handle culturally embedded concepts — references, norms, institutions, and social behaviors that are specific to American life and may not have direct equivalents in other cultures. This domain is intentionally challenging because the concept itself may not map cleanly across languages, not just the words.

**Register:** Neutral to informal, natural prose

**Must include:**
- References to distinctly American institutions, customs, or social norms
- Concepts that would require explanation for someone outside American culture
- Subtle cultural references rather than obvious ones
- A mix of social, legal, financial, and everyday cultural touchpoints

**Must avoid:**
- Globally universal references (Christmas, birthdays, coffee shops)
- Named holidays stated plainly with no cultural context (e.g. "Americans celebrate the Fourth of July")
- Pop culture references tied to a specific year or moment
- Proper nouns for specific cities, states, or brands

**Good examples:**
- She brought a dish to the potluck even though nobody asked her to.
- He took the fifth when they asked him about it at the deposition.
- They argued for twenty minutes about whether to tip fifteen or twenty percent.
- He applied for a 30-year fixed mortgage and got approved the same week.
- She opted out of the company's health plan and went with her spouse's instead.

**Bad examples:**
- Americans celebrate the Fourth of July. (too on the nose, no nuance)
- She went to a café. (no cultural specificity)
- He watched the game on Sunday. (too vague to be culturally meaningful)

### Idiomatic

**Purpose:**
Test how well translators handle fixed figurative expressions whose meaning cannot be derived from the literal meaning of their words. Idioms are one of the hardest categories for machine translation because they require cultural and linguistic knowledge beyond word-level mapping.

**Register:** Natural, contextual — the idiom should be doing real work in the sentence

**Must include:**
- Exactly one idiom per sentence — never stack two idioms
- Enough context around the idiom that its meaning is clear to a native English speaker
- A variety of idioms — do not repeat the same idiom or close variants
- Idioms from different registers and contexts (work, relationships, risk, time, effort)

**Must avoid:**
- Bare idioms with no context ("He bit the bullet." is too sparse)
- Idioms that translate literally and correctly into most major languages — these won't stress-test translation
- Overly obscure idioms that even native speakers might not recognize
- Two idioms in the same sentence
- Idioms so morbid or offensive they distort the dataset tone

**Good examples:**
- She let the cat out of the bag before we could surprise him.
- He's been burning the candle at both ends and it's starting to show.
- They're beating around the bush instead of just saying what they mean.
- He missed the boat on that investment and now he's kicking himself.
- Every cloud has a silver lining — losing that job led him somewhere better.

**Bad examples:**
- He bit the bullet. (no context, too sparse)
- She kicked the bucket yesterday, which was very sad. (morbid, also translates well in many languages)
- He spilled the beans and let the cat out of the bag. (two idioms)

### Technical

**Purpose:**
Test how well translators handle domain-specific technical vocabulary and the precise, formal sentence structures common in professional and technical writing. Technical language is expected to score highest in back-translation fidelity because technical vocabulary is often borrowed across languages or has established equivalents.

**Field:** Software engineering and computer science (maintain consistency across all technical sentences)

**Register:** Formal, precise, professional

**Must include:**
- Real domain-specific vocabulary used correctly
- A mix of conceptual sentences (what something is) and procedural sentences (how something works)
- Passive and active constructions
- Both high-level architecture concepts and lower-level implementation details

**Must avoid:**
- Jargon so niche it only exists in one company's internal documentation
- Sentences that are too vague to be genuinely technical ("The computer processed the data")
- Sentences over 25 words — technical sentences get unwieldy and introduce noise
- Mixed domains (do not blend medical and CS in the same file)

**Good examples:**
- The function returns a null pointer if the input array is empty.
- Asynchronous requests allow the UI to remain responsive during data fetching.
- The query was optimized by adding a composite index on the foreign key.
- Memory leaks occur when allocated resources are not properly released after use.
- The API rate limit resets every hour based on the timestamp of the first request.

**Bad examples:**
- The computer did a thing with the data. (not technical, no real vocabulary)
- She clicked the button. (no technical content)
- The recursive implementation of the Aho-Corasick algorithm leverages a failure function constructed via breadth-first traversal of the trie. (too dense, over 25 words, too niche)

### Legal

**Purpose:**
Test how translators handle formulaic legal language — a register with its own fixed structures, Latin-derived vocabulary, and precise phrasing that varies significantly across legal traditions. Even languages with Latin roots handle legal language very differently, making this a strong stress test for structural fidelity.

**Register:** Formal, precise, legal prose — standard American legal writing style

**Must include:**
- Standard legal phrasing around rights, obligations, agreements, and liability
- A mix of contract language, procedural language, and rights-based language
- Sentences that reflect common American legal concepts
- Both simple declarative legal statements and conditional legal constructions

**Must avoid:**
- Case names or citations
- Jurisdiction-specific references beyond general US context
- Latin phrases — use the English equivalent (use "under oath" not "sub poena")
- Sentences so specialized they only apply to one area of law
- Colloquial restatements of legal concepts — maintain formal register throughout

**Good examples:**
- The defendant waived their right to a jury trial prior to the hearing.
- This agreement shall be governed by the laws of the state of New York.
- The party may terminate the contract with thirty days written notice.
- All parties must disclose any known conflicts of interest before proceedings begin.
- Failure to comply with the terms outlined herein may result in immediate termination of the agreement.

**Bad examples:**
- You have the right to a lawyer. (too colloquial, Miranda-style phrasing)
- The judge decided. (no legal vocabulary or structure)
- Pursuant to the aforementioned sub-clause referenced in exhibit A, the indemnifying party shall... (over 25 words, too dense)

### Emotional / Affective

**Purpose:**
Test how translators handle the expression of internal emotional states — a domain that varies enormously across languages and cultures. Languages differ significantly in how directly, precisely, and grammatically they express emotion, making this a unique and understudied stress test for translation systems.

**Register:** First person, personal, reflective — natural emotional expression

**Must include:**
- First person emotional statements (I feel, I'm, I've been)
- A balance of positive, negative, and ambivalent emotions
- A range of emotional intensities — not all extreme, not all mild
- Nuanced emotional states beyond basic happy/sad/angry
- Both simple emotional declarations and emotionally complex observations

**Must avoid:**
- Clinical or psychological terminology (do not use "depressed," "anxious disorder," etc.)
- Overly dramatic or theatrical phrasing that sounds performed rather than genuine
- Sentences that describe emotions happening to others rather than the speaker
- Clichéd emotional expressions so overused they carry no real signal
- Anything that could be interpreted as expressing crisis or self-harm

**Good examples:**
- I feel completely overwhelmed and I don't know where to start.
- There's something deeply satisfying about finishing a project you've worked on for months.
- I'm not angry, I'm just really disappointed.
- I've been carrying this feeling around for days and I still can't name it.
- It's strange to miss something you're not even sure was ever real.

**Bad examples:**
- I am happy. (too simple, no nuance)
- She felt devastated by the news. (third person, not first)
- I am experiencing significant psychological distress. (clinical register)
- I want to disappear. (potentially crisis-adjacent, avoid)

## Dataset Level Checklist

Before finalizing any domain file:

- [ ] 100+ sentences per domain
- [ ] No duplicate or near-duplicate sentences
- [ ] Sentence lengths vary across the file
- [ ] Topics vary — not all sentences are about the same subject
- [ ] All sentences pass the read-aloud test
- [ ] American English spelling throughout
- [ ] Source logged for any sentences drawn from external references
- [ ] File saved as plain .txt, one sentence per line, no blank lines, no headers
